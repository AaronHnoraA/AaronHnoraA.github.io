Variance

2026-01-30

Summary

In quantum measurement, the variance of an observable M measures how spread out the measurement outcomes are around their expectation value.

For an observable M and a state ∣ψ⟩, the variance is

(ΔM)2=⟨(M−⟨M⟩)2⟩.

Equivalently,

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2.

The standard deviation is

ΔM=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2​.

1. Setup 测量设定

Let M be an observable, so M is a Hermitian operator.

Assume first that M has a discrete non-degenerate spectral decomposition

M=i∑​mi​∣mi​⟩⟨mi​∣,

where

M∣mi​⟩=mi​∣mi​⟩.

Let the system be in a normalized state

∣ψ⟩.

The probability of measuring the outcome mi​ is

pi​=∣⟨mi​∣ψ⟩∣2.

The expectation value of M is

⟨M⟩=i∑​pi​mi​.

Equivalently,

⟨M⟩=⟨ψ∣M∣ψ⟩.

2. Definition of Variance 方差的定义

define

The variance of the observable M in the state ∣ψ⟩ is

(ΔM)2=⟨(M−⟨M⟩I)2⟩.

Since ⟨M⟩ is a scalar, we often write this more simply as

(ΔM)2=⟨(M−⟨M⟩)2⟩.

In terms of measurement outcomes, this is exactly the classical variance:

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​(mi​−⟨M⟩)2.

Here:

  • mi​ is a possible measurement outcome;
  • pi​ is the probability of obtaining mi​;
  • ⟨M⟩ is the average measurement outcome.

3. Main Formula 主公式

Theorem

For any observable M and normalized state ∣ψ⟩,

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2.
Proof

Start from the classical variance formula for measurement outcomes:

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​(mi​−⟨M⟩)2.

Expand the square:

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​(mi2​−2mi​⟨M⟩+⟨M⟩2).

Distribute the sum:

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​mi2​−2⟨M⟩i∑​pi​mi​+⟨M⟩2i∑​pi​.

Now use the two basic facts:

i∑​pi​mi​=⟨M⟩,

and

i∑​pi​=1.

Substituting gives

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​mi2​−2⟨M⟩2+⟨M⟩2.

Therefore,

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​mi2​−⟨M⟩2.

It remains to identify the first term as ⟨M2⟩.

Since

M∣mi​⟩=mi​∣mi​⟩,

we have

M2∣mi​⟩=M(M∣mi​⟩)=M(mi​∣mi​⟩)=mi​M∣mi​⟩=mi2​∣mi​⟩.

Thus ∣mi​⟩ is also an eigenvector of M2, with eigenvalue mi2​.

The probability of obtaining the eigenstate component ∣mi​⟩ is still

pi​=∣⟨mi​∣ψ⟩∣2,

because squaring the operator changes the eigenvalues from mi​ to mi2​, but does not change the eigenvectors.

Hence

⟨M2⟩=i∑​pi​mi2​.

Therefore,

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2.

This proves the formula.

4. Operator Derivation 算符推导

There is also a shorter operator-level derivation.

Proof

By definition,

(ΔM)2=⟨(M−⟨M⟩I)2⟩.

Expand the operator square:

(M−⟨M⟩I)2=M2−2⟨M⟩M+⟨M⟩2I.

Taking expectation values gives

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−2⟨M⟩⟨M⟩+⟨M⟩2⟨I⟩.

Since the state is normalized,

⟨I⟩=⟨ψ∣I∣ψ⟩=⟨ψ∣ψ⟩=1.

Therefore,

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−2⟨M⟩2+⟨M⟩2.

Hence

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2.

5. Degenerate Spectrum 简并谱情形

If M has degenerate eigenvalues, then we should use spectral projections.

Let

M=m∑​mPm​,

where Pm​ is the orthogonal projection onto the eigenspace with eigenvalue m.

Then the probability of obtaining outcome m is

p(m)=⟨ψ∣Pm​∣ψ⟩.

The expectation value is

⟨M⟩=m∑​mp(m).

The second moment is

⟨M2⟩=m∑​m2p(m).

Hence the same variance formula holds:

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2.

6. Density Operator Form 密度算符形式

If the quantum state is represented by a density operator ρ, then

⟨M⟩=tr(ρM),

and

⟨M2⟩=tr(ρM2).

Therefore,

(ΔM)2=tr(ρM2)−tr(ρM)2.

Equivalently,

(ΔM)2=tr(ρ(M−⟨M⟩I)2).

7. Interpretation 物理解释

The variance measures how uncertain the measurement outcomes of M are in the state ∣ψ⟩.

If

(ΔM)2=0,

then the measurement of M is deterministic in that state.

For a pure state, this happens exactly when ∣ψ⟩ lies in an eigenspace of M.

If

(ΔM)2>0,

then repeated measurements of M on identically prepared systems can give different outcomes.

中文直觉：

ΔM

measures the typical size of fluctuation around the mean value

⟨M⟩.

8. Important Special Case: Eigenstates 本征态情形

Suppose

M∣ψ⟩=m∣ψ⟩.

Then measuring M always gives the value m.

Indeed,

⟨M⟩=⟨ψ∣M∣ψ⟩=m,

and

⟨M2⟩=⟨ψ∣M2∣ψ⟩=m2.

Therefore,

(ΔM)2=m2−m2=0.

So eigenstates have zero variance for their corresponding observable.

9. Conceptual Summary 概念总结

Summary

Variance in quantum mechanics is the variance of measurement outcomes.

Starting from the classical statistical formula,

(ΔM)2=i∑​pi​(mi​−⟨M⟩)2,

we obtain the compact operator formula

(ΔM)2=⟨M2⟩−⟨M⟩2.

For a pure state,

⟨M⟩=⟨ψ∣M∣ψ⟩.

For a density operator,

⟨M⟩=tr(ρM).

Thus variance measures the statistical spread of possible measurement outcomes around their expectation value.
